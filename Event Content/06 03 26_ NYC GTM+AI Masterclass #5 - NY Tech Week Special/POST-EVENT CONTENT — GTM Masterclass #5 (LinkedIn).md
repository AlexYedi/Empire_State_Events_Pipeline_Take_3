## LinkedIn Post — Variant A (RECOMMENDED — education-forward, frameworks given room)

Most teams are still doing AI-assisted GTM as a single-player game. One person, hacking alone in Claude Code or Codex, building something nobody else on the team can touch.

That was the quiet diagnosis running under NYC GTM+AI Masterclass #5 at Insight Partners during Tech Week. Five practitioners showed how the work is moving from single-player to multiplayer — and each took a different layer of the shift.

The strategy layer — Sangram Vajre (GTM Partners, co-founder of Terminus). His one-slide GTM Operating System is eight questions a leadership team debates together: market, the value-creating product, a differentiated point of view, motions, time-to-value, expansion, RevOps, alignment. The point isn't the answer. As he put it: "There is no right answer to any of these questions… What this gives you is clarity." His research: 78% of GTM leaders said if they knew what they were doing and why, they'd hit their number. Alignment beats correctness.

The execution layer — Eric Nowoslawski (Growth Engine X, early Clay). His build-your-first-AI-employee playbook is three steps: Context (a company brain in markdown), Connection (read-only access to where work happens), Creation (a suggest → human-approve loop). "I accidentally cut my team by 50%, and we're sending more emails." But copy still passes a human gate every time — "meat gates," he calls them.

The platform layer — Nikita Bokil (Optimizely, ~$400M ARR), on building Opal, an AI harness for ~1,000 marketers. The lesson the industry under-weights: "Treat reliability as a software problem… so quality is provable, not vibes." SLOs, eval gates, observability, agents provisioned as real users with their own identity and audit trail.

The network layer — Nimo Shkedy (NYC GTM Community), host, who named the word of the night: "harnesses." His thesis, said in a room where two other businesses run industrialized cold outbound: "Co-creation is going to replace cold outreach." Qualify by influence, not title.

And the implementation — Kenny Tsai (CoverForce), whose ABM bot narrows a 60,000-person account like Aon down to 12 ranked contacts, collapsing a 4–5 hour setup into 10–15 minutes.

One night, four maturity levels of the same architecture: context, connectors, agents, and a human in the loop. The models are good enough. The work is everything around them.

## LinkedIn Post — Variant B (tighter, sharper POV)

In one room on Tuesday, two businesses sent a combined six figures of cold email a day. A third speaker stood up and said cold outreach is dead.

That tension is the most honest thing I've seen at an AI+GTM event.

At NYC GTM+AI Masterclass #5 (Insight Partners, Tech Week), Eric Nowoslawski (Growth Engine X) and Kenny Tsai (CoverForce) showed cold outbound at industrial scale — Eric running 150 auto-research campaigns at once, Kenny's bot narrowing a 60,000-person account down to 12 ranked contacts in one click. Both lead every first message with a concrete, recent signal. Zero templates.

Then Nimo Shkedy (host, NYC GTM Community) drew the counter-line: "Co-creation is going to replace cold outreach." Stop qualifying by title or company. Qualify by influence — who can introduce you to the people you want to sell to. Map two degrees out: your buyer's commenters' commenters.

Here's the resolution I keep landing on: signals power both. They're the engine of the last era of outbound AND the first era of network plays. The hook is the same; what changes is who's on the other end — a stranger, or someone one warm intro away.

Underneath the disagreement, everyone agreed on the architecture. Sangram Vajre (GTM Partners) framed the strategy: "Go-to-market is the business," owned by the CEO. Nikita Bokil (Optimizely) framed the discipline: reliability for agents is engineering, "provable, not vibes." The models are settled. The fight is now over context and governance.
