## Variant A — "Six founders, six reasons" (portrait gallery)

It takes a particular kind of nerve to stand in front of a room of strangers and explain not just what you built, but why you couldn't *not* build it.

Six founders did exactly that at The Shortlist's August showcase — a hiring night that runs on an almost defiantly old-fashioned premise: the best roles never make it to a job board, so put the founders on a stage and let the room decide who's worth a conversation. Here's who stood up.

**North** (north.cloud) — a financial operating system for cloud, AI, and data spend. Yassine Açoine, the CTO, told the room he'd built his slides "between a crying baby and a very tired wife," then walked through a company born from an argument he kept having with himself. He came up as an engineer running big teams and bigger budgets, watching cloud and AI spend balloon while nobody quite owned it — engineers create the cost, finance answers for it, and no one sits in the middle. North is that middle, freshly expanded last week in its v3 launch to cover Azure alongside native OpenAI, Anthropic, and Snowflake tracking. The detail that stuck: he'd sworn never to hire friends or family, and his co-founder talked him out of it — now some of his best people came from exactly that circle. Hiring across engineering, GTM, CS, and marketing.

**Antimetal** (antimetal.com) — "production that runs itself." Every Antimetal origin story starts at Cafe Select, the SoHo restaurant where CTO Shreyas Iyer met his co-founder Matt for what was meant to be a one-hour lunch. Iyer had been at Meta on the data-center team and, in his telling, was getting bored — realizing infrastructure "sucks just as much at a seven-person company as at Facebook scale," and that there had to be a better way. Antimetal is that better way: agents that build a working model of your systems, find problems, fix them while you sleep, then harden what they touched. They started in cloud cost-optimization (like North), grew fast, then bet the whole company on a harder problem — a call they made not in a boardroom but at a cabin in the woods upstate, the entire five-person team in the room. They're also the ones who once sent a thousand branded pizzas to VCs across two cities ("eight slices to Sequoia, ten to Kleiner Perkins"). $24.3M from Sound Ventures, Nat Friedman, Naval, and the founders of Box and Dropbox. Hiring across engineering, design, and GTM; Grant and Luz were working the room.

**Headstart** (headstart.nyc) — builds production agentic systems (AI that does real multi-step work), deployed one-to-many across private-equity portfolios. "We're all in on the nepotism problem," CEO Nicole Hedley said, nodding at the fact that her co-founder is her brother. An engineer who got early access to the models through a friend at OpenAI, she saw what was coming before most of New York did. What makes Headstart unusual: bootstrapped and profitable from day one, by choice — they had term sheets and turned them down to keep ownership in the team's hands. Her reason was a worldview, not a spreadsheet: the vision is to "free humans up for more fulfilling work," and you can't sell that while running a 996 shop, so it's 40-hour weeks and go-live-your-life. Anthropic wrote a case study on them. Hiring applied-AI PMs and engineers.

**Arist** (arist.co) — agents that automate management consulting. Michael Ioffe opened by asking who in the room had worked at McKinsey, because Arist does what McKinsey does — interview thousands of employees, diagnose what's actually broken, and execute the fix — minus the $5M and the month of guesswork. The origin is the part you don't forget: it began in 2019 as a college project delivering learning into the Yemen war zone, where you couldn't run an online course but you *could* send a text message. Pepsi noticed, and a warzone learning tool became an enterprise platform used by a tenth of the Fortune 500 and the U.S. government. His conviction, earned the hard way: the people closest to the work already know the answers — Starbucks and Intel both faltered, he argued, by paying consultants instead of listening to their own people. Fresh off a $22.5M Series B, backed by UKG, now powering the Department of Labor's AI-literacy program. Hiring generalists who want real ownership — ask Sam, who runs growth nearly solo.

**PointOne** (pointone.com) — AI for the business of law. Jeremy Ben-Meir presented solo; his co-founder Katon couldn't make it, which Jeremy turned into the night's best running joke. Their story is peak Silicon Valley: two friends who met through a mutual friend, one of whom literally moved into the other's closet — tatami mat and all — before they decided they liked working together enough to quit their jobs at Google and Applied Intuition, move upstate (there were goats), and figure out what to build. They landed on the thing every attorney hates and no one had fixed: timekeeping. PointOne turns a lawyer's actual workday into compliant, complete time entries automatically — the business of law, not the practice of it — and has since expanded into bill review and pricing. $16M Series A led by 8VC, with 10x revenue in six months. Hiring engineers; Vicky was there from the team.

**Thunder** (thunder.inc) — AI agents that run B2B paid growth end-to-end. CEO Dilip Rajan has an unusual résumé for an infrastructure founder: he built BuzzFeed's quizzes (yes, those), grew them to billions of views, then founded and sold Jumpcut. Thunder is his bet that the last un-automated corner of go-to-market is paid advertising — agents that don't sell you software but actually run your Google and LinkedIn ad channels, wired straight into your CRM and ad accounts. His framing was the sharpest of the night: unlike a coding agent, where context lives neatly in the repo, growth context is scattered across a dozen tools and every call moves seven- and eight-figure budgets. The team rule — anything they do by hand more than three times, they teach the agents to do. Pre-seed from Company Ventures, whose Matt Harrigan was hosting and called Thunder one of his own. Hiring a founding, forward-deployed growth lead; co-founders Rylan and Nisha were in the back.

The thread across all six: none of them are building AI that *writes*. They're building AI that *runs the thing after it's written* — production, spend, org diagnosis, legal back-office, ad execution. Shreyas Iyer put it plainly: writing code stopped being the bottleneck; operating and maintaining what you shipped is. That's where 2026's roles are being created.

Six founders made their case to a room of strangers. Every one of them is hiring — careers links in the comments. Thanks to Andrew Yeung and The Shortlist for the curation, and Matt Harrigan and Company Ventures for the room.

## Variant B — "The room" (thematic essay)

Watch six founders pitch back-to-back and you stop hearing product descriptions and start hearing worldviews.

That's what The Shortlist's August showcase is really for. It's billed as a hiring night — the premise being that the best roles never reach a job board — but what you actually get is six people explaining why they've organized their lives around one specific problem.

A few are chasing the same ghost from different angles. Yassine Açoine built **North** (north.cloud) — a financial operating system for cloud, AI, and data spend, just expanded in its v3 launch — because as an engineer he watched budgets balloon with nobody owning them. Shreyas Iyer built **Antimetal** (antimetal.com; $24.3M from Sound Ventures, Nat Friedman, Naval, the founders of Box and Dropbox) after realizing at Meta that keeping software alive in production is miserable at any scale; his answer is "production that runs itself." Michael Ioffe built **Arist** (arist.co; fresh off a $22.5M Series B, powering the U.S. Department of Labor's AI-literacy program) to do what McKinsey does — diagnose a broken organization — except by actually interviewing thousands of the people doing the work, a conviction that traces to a 2019 project teaching over text message in the Yemen war zone.

Others are quietly rewriting what their category means. Nicole Hedley's **Headstart** (headstart.nyc) deploys agentic systems one-to-many through private-equity portfolios and is — unusually — bootstrapped and profitable by choice, term sheets declined, because the whole point is to "free humans up for more fulfilling work" and you can't preach that on a 996 schedule. Jeremy Ben-Meir's **PointOne** (pointone.com; $16M Series A, 10x revenue in six months) went after the least glamorous, most hated thing in a law firm — timekeeping — and turned it into an automatic, compliant system for the entire business of law. Dilip Rajan's **Thunder** (thunder.inc), the earliest-stage of the group and pre-seed from host Company Ventures, argues paid growth is the last corner of GTM still run by hand, and builds agents that operate your ad channels rather than sell you a dashboard.

The stories were the part you can't fake. Iyer decided to bet the company at a cabin in the woods with his whole team in the room. Ben-Meir's co-founder once lived in his closet. Hedley's co-founder is her brother ("we're all in on the nepotism problem"). Açoine built his deck between a crying baby and a tired wife. These aren't pitch-deck flourishes — they're why six strangers trusted a room with their purpose.

And the through-line: five of the six aren't building AI that writes — they're building AI that *operates*. Production, spend, org diagnosis, legal ops, ad execution. In 2026 the bottleneck moved from making software to running it, and this room is where that bet is being staffed.

All six are hiring — links in the comments. Thanks to Andrew Yeung and The Shortlist, and to Matt Harrigan and Company Ventures for hosting.
